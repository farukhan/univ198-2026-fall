One Task, Four Ways: The Ladder

The task, all lecture long: prepare next week's seminar for my study group: find three recent readings on our topic, summarise each in five lines, make a one-page handout, and send it to the group. Click a rung to see who does what.

Click a rung.

Same model underneath, often the same app. What changes from left to right is who decides the steps and who executes them. Conventional automation stands beside the ladder, not on it: no model, a rule written once, exact and brittle.

Pick a rung for each, then check one at a time or all four.

Two confusions to avoid. Automation is not an agent: a rule that forwards every invoice decides nothing. A chatbot with a search button is not an agent either: it runs one tool when you ask. The word is earned by the loop.

Activity 1: Chat, Tool, Workflow, Automation or Agent?

Five minutes, on the Google Form. Eight systems; pick the rung for each. Ask one question of each: who chooses the next step: a rule, you, a designer in advance, or the model at run time? Do the form first, then check here, one item or all eight.

For the form: the eight rungs; for two of them, the one word that decided it (rule, text, one call, fixed, loop); and one line on the hardest. Item 6 is the trap: the product calls itself an agent.

What the Room Usually Finds, and What Comes Next

The ends are easy

The spreadsheet rule and the apartment search are placed by nearly everyone. Rules and loops are recognisable.

The middle blurs

Tool-augmented or workflow? The test is who chose the sequence: you, now, or someone, earlier. Half the room swaps them once.

“Agent” is over-used

Item 6 says “agent” in its marketing and reads one document. The word in the product name is not the rung.

The lesson: before you trust, or fear, a system that calls itself an agent, find the loop. If there is none, it is a tool or a script, and you already know how to check those.
