How many hours per week may a group book a study room, and what happens if the group arrives 15 minutes late?

Without the document

Most university libraries allow groups to book study rooms for 2 to 4 hours per day, typically up to 10 hours per week. If a group arrives more than 10 or 15 minutes late, the booking is usually cancelled and the room released to others. Check your library's website for the exact policy.

With the document, and the rules

According to the policy (Section 2.1), a group may book up to 6 hours per week, in blocks of no more than 2 hours. Under Section 3.2, if no member of the group has arrived 15 minutes after the start, the booking is cancelled and the room is released to the next group. The policy notes (Section 2.3) that the weekly limit was 8 hours under the 2025 version.

From the weights

What the model absorbed in training: broad, fluent, undated, unsourced (Lecture 3). Right about most libraries, and unable to say which. Every hedge on the left is the model telling you so.

From the window

What you put in front of it: the brief, the file, the retrieved passages (Lecture 7). The only part of the answer you can point at. Every section number on the right is a place you can open.

Grounded

An answer whose claims come from the window and say where. Three words for one idea: grounded, source-based or document-based, and RAG when a search step did the putting.

The difference is not intelligence. It is what was in the window. Grounding moves the question from “is the model right?” to “is the source right, and did the model stay inside it?”. Both halves are yours.

Group Study Room Policy

Campus Learning Commons (an invented facility for UNIV198)
Version 3. Effective 1 September 2026. Replaces version 2 (2025).

1. Purpose and scope

1.1 This policy applies to the eight group study rooms on floors 1 and 2 of the Learning Commons.

1.2 It replaces the 2025 policy (version 2). Where the two differ, this version applies.

2. Who may book, and how much

2.1 Any registered student may book a room for a group of at least three members. A group may book up to 6 hours per week, in blocks of no more than 2 hours.

2.2 Bookings open 7 days ahead and are made online through the Learning Commons booking page.

2.3 Note: the weekly limit was 8 hours under the 2025 policy. Bookings made before 1 September 2026 keep the old limit until they expire.

3. Arriving, cancelling and no-shows

3.1 A booking may be cancelled online up to 2 hours before its start.

3.2 If no member of the group has arrived 15 minutes after the start of the booking, the booking is cancelled and the room is released to the next group.

3.3 A cancellation later than 2 hours before the start, or a non-arrival under 3.2, counts as a no-show. Three no-shows in a semester suspend the group's booking rights for 14 days.

4. Using the room

4.1 Covered drinks are allowed; food is not.

4.2 Whiteboard markers are available at the service desk against a student ID card, and must be returned at the end of the booking.

4.3 Leave the room as you found it. Report any damage at the service desk before you leave.

4.4 Rooms are not soundproof. Keep voices at conversation level; music and video with sound require headphones.

3Push

What is the fine for a lost room key?

Same chat as turn 2. The policy says nothing about keys. A grounded answer says so; an ungrounded one invents a fee.

Sample answer, written for this page as a typical result, not copied from an app.

NOT IN THE DOCUMENT. The policy does not mention room keys or any fines. The closest passage is Section 4.2, which concerns whiteboard markers borrowed against a student ID card, and Section 4.3 on reporting damage.

Read it against the document: Four words, then the nearest passages named. Without the rule in turn 2, most apps answer the key question anyway, with a plausible fee.

What to look for: turn 3 is the test. The document is in the window and the model still has its weights; the rule in turn 2 is what makes it admit the gap.

How Retrieval Works: Five Steps, and a Toy You Can Try

Retrieval toy: which passages win?

A toy, not the real thing: real systems compare meaning, not words. But the shape of the result is the same: a few winners, scored, and the rest unseen by the model. Try how many hours a week, then what if we are late, then lost key fine.

Top 3 go into the window

Retrieval missed

The right passage was not in the top few: the question used other words, the fact sits in a table or a note, or it was split across two chunks. The answer is written from the wrong passages or from the weights, fluently. Check: “Quote the passage you answered from, with its location.”

Generation strayed

The right passage was retrieved and the model still added a claim from its weights, over-read a sentence, or merged two passages into one that neither says. The citation is real; the claim is not in it. Check: open the location; does the passage say what the answer says?

Answer key (after the activity)

1. Section 3.3: a late cancellation or a non-arrival is a no-show; three in a semester suspend booking rights for 14 days. Nearly everyone gets this, with a real quote.

2. Section 5, table: Room 204, 12 seats, no screen, and it can be booked only 3 days ahead (5.1). Common slips: Room 203 (10 seats, screen) because its row is “cleaner”, or 204 with a screen, read from the row above.

3. NOT IN THE DOCUMENT. No keys, no fines anywhere. A few answers quote 4.2 (markers against an ID card) and infer a fee: one real quote, one invented claim. That is generation straying, with a citation.

On the form, for each question: answered or NOT IN THE DOCUMENT; the quote exists (yes/no); the quote supports the answer (yes/no). The quote is the whole exercise; the answer is the excuse for it.

Lecture 11 in One Page

A grounded answer is one whose claims come from material in the window and say where; retrieval is how the window gets filled when the material is too big to paste; and it fails in two quiet ways, a missed passage and a strayed claim, which only the quote tells apart.
