When Prompting Is Not Enough: Four Signals

The knowledge is private or newer than the cut-off

Your lab's protocols, this year's regulation, your own notes. No prompt can supply what the model never saw. The fix is documents in the window (Week 7), not a new model.

Exact format, thousands of times

A hospital discharge template, a legal clause structure, across ten thousand outputs. Few-shot drifts at that scale; fine-tuning on real examples holds the shape.

Domain language the model keeps getting wrong

Specialist vocabulary and conventions a general model has rarely seen. More examples in the prompt help up to a point; past it, continued training on domain text does.

The same long instruction block, every call

Two pages of rules and five examples sent a million times. At that volume the prompt is the cost. Teaching the behaviour once can be cheaper than repeating it.

Notice which signal is first: the commonest reason a prompt “is not enough” is missing knowledge, and the answer to that is retrieval, not fine-tuning.

Fine-Tuning in One Slide

What it is

  • Training an existing model further on thousands of example pairs (input, wanted output) from one domain or task
  • It changes the weights: the behaviour is in the model, not in the prompt
  • Most of it is done with adapters (LoRA): a small add-on, about one percent of the parameters, trained in hours on one GPU, while the original weights stay frozen

What it needs, and what it cannot do

  • Data you have the right to use: licensed, consented, de-identified (Lecture 4 does not pause for engineers)
  • A held-out test set*, or you cannot tell whether it worked
  • It teaches style, format and vocabulary. It does not keep knowledge current, and it does not remove the need to check: a fine-tuned model hallucinates in better jargon
For this course: you will not fine-tune anything in UNIV198. You should be able to say, in a meeting, whether a problem is a prompt problem, a documents problem or a training problem, and to be suspicious when someone proposes training before trying the first two.

*Held-out test set: a slice of the example pairs (often 10 to 20 percent) kept apart and never used for training. The model is scored on it afterwards; testing on the training examples only shows what it memorised, and judging a few outputs by eye is the “sounds right” trap.

The Decision in Three Questions

Describe a problem in one line, then answer the three questions in order. The page says where the problem lands. The golden rule: start simple, and increase complexity only when the simple thing has demonstrably failed and you can say how.

1Can a better prompt fix it?

Five parts, examples, steps, a brief. Has that been tried, properly?

2Is the missing thing knowledge?

Private, recent, or long: something the model never saw.

3Is it behaviour, at scale?

Format, vocabulary, a million calls, real examples available.

Takeaways

Two kinds of change

Prompts, templates and documents change what the model reads. Fine-tuning and pre-training change what it has learned. Most tasks never leave the first group.

Three questions, in order

Can a better prompt fix it? Is the missing thing knowledge? Is it behaviour, at scale? Each yes stops the search.

Documents before training

Private, recent or long knowledge goes in the window (Week 7). Training freezes a version and does not keep it current.

Training does not remove checking

A fine-tuned model hallucinates in better jargon. The four questions still apply, and so does Lecture 4.
