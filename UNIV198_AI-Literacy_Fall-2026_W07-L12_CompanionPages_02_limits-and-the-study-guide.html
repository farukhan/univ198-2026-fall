Where Retrieval Stops, and What to Do Instead

Every row has the same shape: retrieval hands the model a sample; the question needed the whole. When it fits, paste the whole; when it does not, ask in pieces and assemble the answer yourself. Then sort the eight requests below.

The edgeWhyInstead

The Web as a Document: Search-Grounded Answers

With search on, the app retrieves a few pages and answers from them: Lecture 11's five steps with the web as the store. The links are the citations and the three gates apply unchanged. One thing is new: you did not choose the documents, so a fourth gate joins the test: is the source worth citing?

Rate a cited page

For one link under a search-grounded answer, answer the four questions. The verdict is a habit, not a formula.

Who wrote it?

When?

Why does the page exist?

Does the page itself say what the answer says?

What is different on the web

  • The retrieved page may itself be wrong, old, or an advertisement. A perfect citation to a bad page is a grounded claim about a bad page.
  • An AI overview on a search page is the same mechanism, made by the search engine; the links under it are the grounding.
  • “Not there” almost never happens: the web always has something near the question, so the invented answer comes better dressed.

The habit

Open two of the links before you believe the summary. Read the page, not the snippet. A page that fails the fourth gate makes the claim ungrounded for your coursework, however good the quote. Mike Caulfield's SIFT is the same idea in four moves: stop, investigate the source, find better coverage, trace to the original.

Disclosing Grounded Work: The Line Builder

A reader of grounded work needs to know what was in the window, because that is what the answer is about. Five parts, about thirty words. Fill the parts; the line assembles below. “Checked” means a number; “changed” is the proof.

Three tests for the line

  • Could a reader tell what was in the window? (tool, date, documents and versions)
  • Could a reader tell how much you checked? (a number, not “all checked”)
  • Could a reader tell what you changed? (a line with no changes needs a reason)

Activity 3, Take-Home: A Study Guide With Page Numbers

This week, one submission each, on the Google Form. From your three documents, a one-page study guide of at most ten claims for one course; every claim carries a document and page. Open and check five of the ten with the citation test; correct or remove what fails; add the disclosure line. Marked for the five checks and the line, not for the guide.

1. The prompt

Ask for the ten most useful claims, not “a summary”: a summary is a whole-document question.

Before you upload

Your own notes, the readings the course posted, published papers, public regulations: in. A classmate's annotated copy, a lecturer's unpublished slides, anything confidential: not without agreement, and never into a notebook with memory on. Lecture 4, for every file.

2. Five checks

For each of five claims: where it points, the three verdicts in order, what you did.

3. The line

Build it on slide 3, or write it here: tool, date, documents and versions, every claim cited, five of ten checked, what changed.

Two failures out of five is a normal result; zero failures with no evidence of checking is not. A guide with two honest corrections scores higher than a flawless one with “all checked” and nothing to show for it.
