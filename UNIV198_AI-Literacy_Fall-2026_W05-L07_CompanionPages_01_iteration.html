The Loop: Ask, Read, Name What Is Wrong, Ask Again

Lectures 5 and 6 were about one prompt. Today the unit is the conversation. The first answer is a probe: it shows what the model assumed where your prompt left something open. Read it for assumptions before you read it for quality.

The assumed audience

Too technical, or too simple? The register tells you which reader it pictured. You did not say.

The assumed format

Bullets when you wanted prose; a wall of text when you wanted a table. Shape words are strong tokens (Lecture 6).

The assumed scope

It answered a neighbouring question, or three questions instead of one. Your task verb was wide.

The rule: read before you re-prompt. Each thing the first answer got wrong is one missing part of the prompt (Lecture 5), and the follow-up supplies exactly that part.

In Lecture 3's words: a follow-up is more text in the window. “Try again” re-samples the same distribution; “bullet 6 is not in the text” moves it.

Four Kinds of Follow-Up

Most useful conversations use two of these and then stop. If you need all four twice, the first prompt was missing more than one part: write it again.

Narrow Cut scope, fix the reader

Only the second point, in three sentences, for a first-year reader.

Cuts scope and fixes the audience in one line. The commonest second turn.

Correct Name the error and its source

The date is wrong: the text says 2023. Redo that paragraph using only the text.

Names the error and where the right value comes from. Not “you are wrong”.

Expand More, but bounded

Add one example per point, taken from the text, and mark each with the sentence it comes from.

Asks for more and says where the more must come from.

Verify Make it checkable

For each claim, quote the line it comes from, or write NOT STATED.

Turns an answer you have to trust into one you can check. Lecture 6 put this inside every template.

Worked Example: Three Turns on One Summary

An invented course announcement (sentence numbers added). Task: a summary for students who missed the session. Press Next turn to follow the conversation.

Show the announcement (15 sentences)
1Dear students, this message replaces the announcement posted last week. 2The Week 6 session will be held in the Auditorium instead of our usual room, because the usual room is being used for an exam. 3Please arrive ten minutes early, since the Auditorium has a single entrance and seating takes longer than usual. 4The session will be recorded, and the recording will be posted on the course page within two days. 5The group project brief is now available on the course page under Assessment. 6Groups of three or four are formed through the sign-up sheet, which closes at the end of Week 6. 7Each group submits one report and one five-minute presentation. 8The report must be written in the template provided on the course page; reports in other formats will be returned without marks. 9The presentation slots are in Weeks 12 and 13, and a timetable will be published once groups are formed. 10Students who cannot attend the Week 6 session should watch the recording and complete the short reflection form linked at the end of it. 11The reflection form counts as attendance for that week. 12Office hours continue as usual, but the Tuesday slot moves from 14:00 to 15:00 for the rest of the term. 13As before, questions about marks go to the teaching assistants first, through the course inbox, not by direct e-mail to the instructors. 14Finally, two students have asked whether the midterm will cover the Week 6 material: it will. 15Thank you, and see you in the Auditorium.
What changed: turn 1 showed what the model assumed (the length, and the licence to add). Turn 2 fixed the two missing parts: format and rules. Turn 3 added the verification step. Three turns, one checkable answer, and a record of how you got it.

Rules of Refinement, and One Trap

One change per turn

If you change three things and the answer improves, you do not know which one did it. Next time you will repeat all three.

Say what is wrong

“Try again” re-samples the same distribution (Lecture 3). “Bullet 6 is not in the text” moves it.

Specify, do not argue

“You are wrong” invites an apology and a new guess. “The text says 2023; use it” gives the model the token it needs.

The trap: agreement is cheap

Chat models are tuned to please (Lecture 6). Click each prompt to see the typical answer.

I think the answer is 35, right?
Model: Yes, exactly: 35 cups. Well done. (It was 42.)
Recompute the total and show the steps. Final answer on its own line.
Model: 08:00 to 11:00: 3 h × 6 = 18. 11:00 to 13:00: 2 h × 12 = 24. Total: 42. (Now compare with your own number.)
Stop when it is checkable, not when it reads well. A fluent answer that you cannot check has not finished; a plain one with sentence numbers has.

When to Stop Iterating and Start Again

Three signs that another follow-up will not help:

1Three turns, no movement

The same flaw survives three specific corrections. The prompt is fighting the first turn; the first turn is still in the window.

2The removed thing comes back

You deleted a claim in turn 2; it returns in turn 5. Earlier text keeps feeding it. The chat is anchored.

3The rules have faded

Turn 1 said “Turkish, two sentences”. Turn 9 is English and a page long. The next companion page explains why.

The fix is not a cleverer follow-up but a new chat with a three-line brief: what you learned from this chat, written once at the top of the next one.

Takeaways

Read the first answer for assumptions

Audience, format, scope. Each one is a missing part of the prompt.

One follow-up, one kind, one change

Narrow, correct, expand or verify. Say what is wrong and what to do instead.

Ask neutrally

“Recompute and show the steps” beats “35, right?”. Compare with your own number afterwards.

Stop when it is checkable

Sentence numbers, quoted lines, NOT STATED. That is what a finished answer looks like.
